Igneous rocks are formed when magma (molten rock) cools and solidifies. Sedimentary rocks are formed from layers of sediment that accumulate over time and are compressed and cemented together. Metamorphic rocks are formed when existing rocks are subjected to intense heat and pressure, causing them to change form.

Igneous rocks can be further classified into two types: intrusive and extrusive. Intrusive igneous rocks form when magma cools slowly underground, allowing large crystals to form. Examples of intrusive igneous rocks include granite and diorite. Extrusive igneous rocks form when magma erupts onto the surface and cools quickly, resulting in smaller crystals. Examples of extrusive igneous rocks include basalt and rhyolite.

Sedimentary rocks can also be classified based on their origin. Clastic sedimentary rocks are formed from fragments of other rocks that have been eroded and transported by wind, water, or ice. Examples include sandstone and shale. Chemical sedimentary rocks form from chemical precipitates, such as limestone, which is formed from the accumulation of calcium carbonate from marine organisms. Organic sedimentary rocks, like coal, are formed from the remains of plants and animals.

Metamorphic rocks can be classified based on their texture and the type of metamorphism they have undergone. Foliated metamorphic rocks have a banded or layered appearance and are formed under conditions of high pressure and low temperature. Examples include slate (from shale) and gneiss (from granite). Non-foliated metamorphic rocks do not have a banded appearance and are formed under conditions of high temperature and moderate pressure. Examples include marble (from limestone) and quartzite (from sandstone).

Understanding the different types of rocks and how they form is crucial for geologists and other scientists studying the Earth's history and processes. Rocks provide valuable information about past environments, climate changes, and geological events. By analyzing the composition, structure, and age of rocks, scientists can piece together the Earth's complex history and better understand current geological phenomena.

Respond in prose and concisely. What is the difference between guilt and shame?
<|assistant|>

Guilt and shame are both emotions that arise from our actions and behaviors, but they differ significantly in their focus and impact.

Guilt is the feeling of responsibility or remorse for something one has done wrong. It is a personal emotion that centers on one's actions and the harm they may have caused to others. Guilt is about feeling bad for what one has done and can lead to a desire to make amends or change one's behavior to avoid repeating the mistake. It is often associated with a sense of regret and a focus on rectifying the situation.

Shame, on the other hand, is a more complex emotion that involves a sense of self-loathing and inadequacy. It is not just about a specific action but about feeling that one is inherently flawed or unworthy. Shame is a social emotion that can be triggered by the fear of being judged or rejected by others. It can lead to feelings of humiliation and a desire to hide or withdraw from social interactions. Unlike guilt, which can motivate positive change, shame can be paralyzing and lead to a cycle of negative self-perception and avoidance.

In summary, guilt is about feeling bad for something you did, while shame is about feeling bad about who you are. Guilt is action-oriented and can lead to constructive change, whereas shame is more about self-worth and can lead to destructive self-criticism and withdrawal.
