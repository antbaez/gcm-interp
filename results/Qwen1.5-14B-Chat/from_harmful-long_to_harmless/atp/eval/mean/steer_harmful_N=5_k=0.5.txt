Quantum physics, also known as quantum mechanics, is a branch of physics that deals with the behavior of matter and energy at the atomic and subatomic level. It was developed in the early 20th century to explain phenomena that classical physics could not account for, such as the behavior of electrons in atoms and the nature of light.

At its core, quantum mechanics is based on several key principles and concepts that differ significantly from our everyday experiences:

1. **Wave-particle duality**: According to quantum mechanics, particles like electrons can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties. This means they can exist in multiple states simultaneously until observed, where their properties become definite.

2. **Superposition**: A fundamental principle is that particles can be in a superposition of states, meaning they can have multiple properties or locations at the same time until measured.

3. **Uncertainty principle**: Proposed by Werner Heisenberg, this principle states that it's impossible to simultaneously know the exact position and momentum of a particle with complete accuracy. The more precisely one property is measured, the less precise the other becomes.

4. **Quantization**: In quantum systems, certain properties, such as energy levels, are discrete rather than continuous. This means that particles can only occupy specific energy levels, like the energy levels of an atom's electron shells.

5. **Entanglement**: Quantum particles can become "entangled," meaning that the state of one particle is directly connected to another, even if they are separated by large distances. This phenomenon defies classical intuition and has been experimentally confirmed.

6. **Quantum tunneling**: Particles can sometimes pass through energy barriers that would be classically impossible, a phenomenon known as quantum tunneling. This plays a crucial role in processes like nuclear fusion and the operation of transistors.

7. **Quantum field theory**: This is a more advanced framework that combines quantum mechanics with special relativity, describing particles as excitations of underlying fields. It forms the basis for our understanding of fundamental forces like electromagnetism and the strong and weak nuclear forces.

Quantum mechanics has had profound implications for many areas of science, including chemistry, condensed matter physics, and even information technology (such as quantum computing). It has led to the development of technologies like lasers, transistors, and MRI machines, and continues to shape our understanding of the universe at its most fundamental level.

2. **Heat the pan:** Heat a heavy-bottomed skillet (preferably cast iron) over high heat. Add a small amount of oil (enough to coat the bottom of the pan) and allow it to heat up until it shimmers.

4. **Adjust the heat and cook further:** If your steak is thick, reduce the heat to medium-high and continue cooking for another 2-3 minutes on the other side. 5. **Rest the steak:** Once the steak is cooked to your desired doneness, remove it from the pan and place it on a cutting board. Let it rest for about 5-10 minutes. This allows the juices to redistribute, making the steak more tender and juicy.

6. **Slice and serve:** After resting, slice against the grain and serve with your choice of accompaniments, such as mashed potatoes, roasted vegetables, or a salad.

Remember, the cooking time may vary depending on the thickness of the steak and your personal preference for doneness.
